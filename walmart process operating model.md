# Walmart — Process / Operating Model Lead Brief

*Grounded in the Customer — Retail | Role: Process / Operating Model Lead*
*Prepared: October 28, 2026*

> **Role prompt:** "How is AI changing replenishment, fulfillment, fraud/returns, and labor scheduling?"

Source numbers in [brackets] refer to the Sources list at the end. Walmart's own sources are used wherever they exist. **Gap to know up front:** Walmart has published very little about AI in returns and fraud (see Section 3).

---

## The Operating-Model Answer in 60 Seconds

Walmart is moving from planning cycles to a continuous loop. Stores now act as fulfillment hubs, automated distribution centers feed them, and AI tools assign the work. The business result is growth without matching growth in headcount or inventory.

- **Inventory discipline:** In fiscal 2026, inventory increased 2.6%, about half the rate of sales growth for the full year. [11]
- **The open question:** Can store associates, return processes and fraud controls keep pace with the automation?

---

## 1. Replenishment: From Forecast-and-Wait to Sense-and-Respond

- **Self-correcting inventory:** Walmart's system is designed to detect inventory imbalances and automatically redirect products toward locations where they are more likely to be needed. Walmart says its self-healing inventory technology in Mexico has generated more than $55 million in savings. [14]
- **Store-sequenced pallets:** Pallets are sequenced based on what individual stores need; urgent items are loaded last so they unload first. As a result, store teams unload some trailers in minutes instead of hours. [13]
- **Specialized AI models:** Walmart uses external frontier AI models where appropriate, while also developing specialized models for tasks such as demand forecasting and route optimization. [16]
- **Shifting inventory risk to sellers:** 52% of marketplace sellers use Walmart Fulfillment Services. Instead of carrying this stock, Walmart provides fulfillment and customer access, earning fees while shifting working-capital and markdown risk to sellers. [11]

**Tension to probe:** Q2 FY27 inventory reached $61.6B, up $3.9B or 6.7% [3] — faster than sales growth, reversing the fiscal 2026 discipline story. Is this deliberate stock placed for speed, or a forecasting miss?

---

## 2. Fulfillment: The Store Becomes a Fulfillment Node

- **Scale:** In Q2 FY27, deliveries fulfilled from local stores increased 43%, and 3,100 U.S. stores are now served with some level of automated freight. [6]
- **Speed:** Expedited deliveries of less than three hours represented about 37% of store-fulfilled orders. [7]
- **Back-of-store automation:** Each SymMicro station can support 550 picks per hour and helps limit in-store traffic generated by e-commerce pickup. The first unit is only now being installed (see Section 5). [8]
- **Earlier results:** Walmart cut U.S. net delivery costs per order by 40% in Q4 FY26. [9]

**Operating-model implication:** Store-level in-stock performance and digital performance are no longer separate conversations. As automation penetration increases, the tolerance for variability inside the system decreases. [10]

---

## 3. Fraud and Returns: The Least Public, Most Exposed Area

**Walmart-specific evidence**
- **Agent purchases:** Walmart's new terms for AI agents say customers are in control, and Walmart's returns process applies — including if an agent doesn't act as intended. Customers must approve agent purchases by default. [2]
- **Regulatory history (money transfers, not merchandise returns):** The FTC alleged Walmart failed to put effective anti-fraud policies and procedures in place, did not properly train employees, and failed to warn customers about possible fraud related to money transfers. These are the FTC's allegations, which Walmart settled in June 2025. [12]

**Industry context (not Walmart's own figures)**
- **Fraud rate:** About 9% of returns are fraudulent (NRF and Happy Returns, 2025 Retail Returns Landscape report). [5]
- **Fake damage photos:** Shoppers are submitting AI-generated images of damaged or incorrect products to claim refunds on merchandise received in good condition. [17]
- **AI isn't enough alone:** Only 45% of companies believe AI and machine learning are truly effective at preventing return fraud on their own. [5]

**Gap to flag openly:** No public Walmart figures were found on AI-driven returns or fraud detection. This belongs in the "must validate" column — and it's the best discovery opening.

## 4. Labor Scheduling and Store Execution: AI as the Work Assigner

- **Walmart's claim:** AI-powered tools for U.S. associates include real-time translation and task management, reducing shift planning time from 90 minutes to 30. [1]
- **Platform:** All Walmart's AI tools are powered by its proprietary machine learning platform, Element. [1]
- **Reality on the floor:** Employees often spend time training the software, supplying the context and judgment AI agents lack. [4]
  - **Tasks:** Walmart uses AI assistants like Sidekick to assign tasks and targets for employees. [4]
  - **Picking routes:** One fulfillment worker said of AI-projected pick times, "There's no way to win on those." [4]
- **Shareholder pressure:** Shareholders rejected a June 4 proposal asking for a report on how AI affects workforce well-being. [15]

**Tension to probe:** If the software saves an hour, does that hour come back as better staffing, steadier schedules, or simply a faster pace? [15]

---

## 5. Gaps Between Strategy and Execution (Worksheet A)

1. **Inventory discipline vs. inventory growth.** Fiscal 2026 showed lean inventory [11], but Q2 FY27 inventory grew 6.7%. [3]
2. **Automated distribution centers vs. manual stores.** The upstream network is automated, but in-store automation is unproven. Symbotic expects the first in-store system to "come to life" in about six months; its CEO said more sites should follow "once Walmart actually sees the system working." [8]
3. **Faster agent shopping vs. returns exposure.** Walmart now accepts returns on orders AI agents place in error [2], but has shared no plan for returns risk.
4. **"People-led" vs. AI-set work targets.** Productivity gains rely on associates' buy-in, while frontline workers report targets they can't hit. [4]

## 6. Bold Discovery Pack (Worksheet C)

### Provocations
- "Your distribution centers run on robots, but your stores still run on people correcting AI. Where does throughput actually break?"
- "If agents can order in error and return for free, who owns the cost of agent mistakes: digital, stores, or finance?"
- "What would it mean to treat returns as a data product — feeding replenishment and fraud signals — instead of a cost center?" *(adapted from the training deck, slide 5)*

### Discovery Questions (top 3 marked ★)
1. ★ Is the Q2 inventory build stock placed deliberately for speed, or a forecast miss? Which categories?
2. ★ Who owns the full returns process across stores, eCommerce, marketplace and agent orders — and is there one view of returns risk?
3. ★ Where do the 60 minutes saved per scheduling cycle go: more staffing, steadier schedules, or a faster pace?
4. What are the go/no-go criteria for scaling in-store pickup automation beyond the first sites?
5. How is the inventory-rebalancing approach that saved $55M+ in Mexico being brought to U.S. stores, and what's blocking it?
6. How do you measure the accuracy of AI-assigned tasks and pick routes, and who can override them?
7. How do marketplace and Walmart Fulfillment Services sellers fit into your returns and fraud controls?
8. Which store metrics have changed now that stores act as fulfillment nodes?
9. What did you change in fraud controls after the FTC money-transfer settlement?
